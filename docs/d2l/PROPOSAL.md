## Draft case-study copy

The following is **draft portfolio prose for review**, not approved or implemented content. Source mapping and limitations stay in the analysis; later figure captions supply concise node references. Proposed evaluation is explicitly future work.

### Introduction

**D2L Redesign — Making academic workflows easier to navigate.**

How might we make everyday academic tasks easier for students and instructors? Our team examined usability challenges in D2L, mapped key coursework workflows, and explored redesigned interfaces for submitting and managing assignments. Working collaboratively, we developed concepts for both student and instructor experiences.

**Project-status note:** A collaborative Michigan State University redesign proposal, not an official D2L release. The work presents evaluation notes and prototype decisions; measured usability improvements have not been established.

### 01 — The Challenge

Academic work involves more than opening a course page. Students need to find an assignment, understand its requirements, choose a file and know whether it was submitted. Instructors need to organize those requirements, define grading criteria and review work.

We focused the redesign on these connected tasks. The opportunity was to make the context, available actions and next state easier to see as someone moved through coursework.

### 02 — Evaluating the Existing Experience

Our heuristic evaluation recorded three themes: navigation and information hierarchy, control over submitted files, and the structure of assignment and rubric creation.

The notes described missing current-page cues and differences between course layouts. They also raised uncertainty about which file should count as the final submission and difficulty distinguishing cells in the rubric editor. Proposed responses included clearer headings and navigation states, better control over files, and more distinct rubric sections.

These observations gave us concrete design questions to explore rather than a reason to replace every part of the interface.

### 03 — Mapping Student & Instructor Workflows

For students, we mapped the journey from selecting a course to reviewing a chosen file and receiving submission confirmation. Treating file selection, review and submission as separate states made the intended sequence visible.

The instructor flow connected assignment details, grading criteria and supporting files with publishing, grading and feedback. Looking at both journeys kept the work focused on coursework as a shared process. The diagrams capture the intended paths; alternative and error states would need further exploration.

### 04 — Exploring Design Solutions

Early sketches established course, assignment and upload layouts before the visual direction was resolved. The later designs used a green-and-white palette, repeated navigation labels and grouped content areas.

Some iterations changed how much information the screens contained more than how they were arranged. The final assignment view, for example, retained the earlier structure while adding populated directions and comment-editor controls. Showing these specific changes makes the progression clearer than presenting every version as a complete redesign.

### 05 — The Redesigned Experience

The student concept keeps assignment context visible through a named heading and a summary of due date, points, submission type and status. A file-selection sequence leads to a review state with the chosen filename, a removal control and Submit. Separate confirmation and history views explore how completion could be communicated.

For instructors, the proposal separates assignment overview from grading and submission settings. The rubric maker labels criteria, headers and score, while a review screen gathers assignment settings before publishing.

These screens express the team's intended experience. The confirmation and underlying status do not yet agree in every state, and the inspected prototype connections do not establish a complete production workflow.

### 06 — Reflection & Next Steps

The project connects evaluation of an existing product with the decisions needed to find, submit and manage coursework. Its strongest contribution is the relationship between those tasks and the interface states the team explored.

Future evaluation should test whether people understand the navigation, can recover from choosing the wrong file, and can distinguish saving, submitting and publishing. The same attention should extend to keyboard use, screen-reader feedback and readable status information.

**Proposed priorities:**

- **Validate the student journey:** assignment discovery, file selection, removal, submission and history; resolve contradictory/empty states and prototype links.
- **Evaluate instructor setup:** date meanings, grading/rubric choices, draft versus publish, and feedback delivery.
- **Review accessibility and recovery:** focus order, dialog behavior, labels, contrast, status announcements, unsupported files, upload failure and resubmission.
